Across Atlantic Canada, a generation of owners is getting ready to step back. The Atlantic Provinces Economic Council (APEC) estimates that roughly 25,000 small businesses in the region will change hands or close as their owners retire, including about 7,900 self-employed business owners in New Brunswick alone.

Most of the conversation about this focuses on valuation, tax and buyers. Those matter. But in my experience, the thing that makes or breaks a transition is people.

## Your value walks out the door every evening

In many owner-led businesses, the most valuable assets aren't on the balance sheet. They're the customer relationships the owner has built over decades, the supervisor who knows how everything really works, and the culture that makes good people stay. A buyer or successor is paying for those as much as anything else.

If those relationships and that knowledge live in one or two heads, the business is worth less, and it's harder to transfer.

## What a people-first succession plan covers

**Who can lead.** An honest look at your internal candidates, what they're ready for today and what they need to be ready in two or three years.

**Key-person risk.** Which roles would hurt most to lose, and how to reduce that risk through development, documentation and cross-training.

**Relationship transfer.** A plan for introducing your successor to customers, suppliers and community partners, well before you leave. APEC's own advice is to plan within the last five years of your career, not at the last minute.

**Retention.** Making sure the people who carry the business stay through the transition, and know where they fit after it.

## Start earlier than feels necessary

Developing a leader takes longer than most owners expect. If you're thinking about stepping back in the next five years, the best time to start the people plan is now. Your accountant and lawyer will handle the deal. Make sure someone is looking after the people who make it worth doing.
